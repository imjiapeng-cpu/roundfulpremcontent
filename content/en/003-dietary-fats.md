# Dietary Fat: What's Actually Good for You

For decades, dietary fat was cast as a primary villain in nutritional health — something to be minimised, reduced, and avoided. That picture has since been substantially revised. The evidence now tells a more nuanced story: fat is an essential macronutrient that serves critical biological functions, and the *type* of fat matters far more than the total amount. Understanding the different categories of dietary fat — and what the research actually says about each — is foundational to making good food choices.

## Why your body needs fat

Fat is not optional. It serves multiple essential roles in human physiology:

**Energy storage and supply.** Fat is the body's most calorie-dense energy source, providing 9 calories per gram (compared to 4 for carbohydrates and protein). It is the primary fuel source for lower-intensity, sustained activity.

**Cell membrane structure.** Every cell in the body is enclosed in a bilipid membrane whose composition and fluidity depend on the fatty acids you eat.

**Hormone production.** Sex hormones, including testosterone and oestrogen, as well as steroid hormones, are synthesised from cholesterol — a type of fat produced by the body and consumed in food.

**Fat-soluble vitamin absorption.** Vitamins A, D, E, and K are fat-soluble, meaning they require dietary fat to be absorbed from food and transported through the body. Without adequate fat in the diet, these vitamins cannot be properly utilised even if consumed.

**Brain function.** The brain is nearly 60% fat by dry weight. Fatty acids — particularly omega-3s — are critical for neural development, synaptic function, and long-term cognitive health.

**Inflammation regulation.** Certain dietary fats are precursors to prostaglandins and other signalling molecules that regulate inflammatory responses throughout the body.

The question, then, is not whether to eat fat — but which fats, in what quantities, and in what balance.

## The main types of dietary fat

### Saturated fats

Saturated fats are solid at room temperature and are found predominantly in animal products — red meat, butter, cheese, full-fat dairy, and poultry skin — as well as in some tropical plant oils, particularly coconut oil and palm oil.

Decades of research have consistently linked high saturated fat intake to elevated LDL ("bad") cholesterol levels. Raised LDL is a well-established risk factor for atherosclerosis (arterial plaque buildup) and cardiovascular disease. Current guidance from the American Heart Association recommends limiting saturated fat to less than 10% of total daily calories, with some guidelines recommending under 7% for individuals at elevated cardiovascular risk.

The relationship is more complex than once believed, however. Not all saturated fats behave identically: stearic acid (found in beef and dark chocolate) appears largely neutral with respect to LDL, while lauric acid (prominent in coconut oil) raises both LDL and HDL simultaneously. Dietary context also matters — replacing saturated fat with refined carbohydrates, as many low-fat dietary approaches inadvertently did, does not appear to improve cardiovascular outcomes, while replacing it with unsaturated fats does.

### Unsaturated fats

Unsaturated fats are liquid at room temperature and are broadly considered the most health-favourable fats in the diet. They divide into two main categories.

**Monounsaturated fats (MUFAs)** are found in olive oil, avocados, most nuts (especially almonds, cashews, and hazelnuts), and certain seeds. They have been consistently associated with reduced LDL cholesterol, maintained or improved HDL levels, and reduced cardiovascular risk. The Mediterranean diet — high in olive oil and associated with strong longevity and cardiovascular outcomes in multiple large studies — is largely built around monounsaturated fat as the primary fat source.

**Polyunsaturated fats (PUFAs)** include two particularly important subcategories: omega-6 and omega-3 fatty acids. These are termed "essential" fatty acids because the human body cannot synthesise them from scratch — they must come from food.

- **Omega-6 fatty acids** are found in vegetable oils (sunflower, corn, soybean), seeds, and many processed foods. They are important for cellular signalling but should be consumed in reasonable proportion to omega-3s. Modern Western diets tend toward heavily omega-6-dominant ratios, which may influence inflammatory balance.

- **Omega-3 fatty acids** are found in fatty fish (salmon, mackerel, sardines, herring), flaxseeds, chia seeds, and walnuts. Omega-3s — particularly EPA and DHA from marine sources — are among the most well-supported nutrients in nutritional science. They are anti-inflammatory, protective of cardiovascular function, critical for brain and eye development, and associated with reduced risk of cardiovascular events. The plant-based form (ALA) must be converted to EPA and DHA in the body, but this conversion is limited in efficiency, making direct marine or algae-based sources preferable for those who rely on them.

### Trans fats

Trans fats are the one category of dietary fat with a near-unanimous research consensus: industrially produced trans fats are harmful and should be avoided.

Naturally occurring trans fats exist in small amounts in the meat and dairy of ruminant animals (cattle, sheep) and appear relatively benign at typical intake levels. The concern is with *industrially produced* trans fats — also called partially hydrogenated oils — which were widely used in processed foods, commercial baked goods, margarine, and fried foods throughout much of the 20th century due to their low cost, long shelf life, and cooking stability.

Industrial trans fats simultaneously raise LDL cholesterol and lower HDL cholesterol — a particularly harmful combination for cardiovascular risk. They are also associated with systemic inflammation. At peak consumption, they were estimated to contribute to hundreds of thousands of premature cardiovascular deaths globally each year. Many countries have since banned or severely restricted their use in the food supply. However, they may still appear in imported or processed foods under ingredient labels reading "partially hydrogenated oil" — worth checking before purchasing.

## How much fat should you eat?

Current evidence supports fat comprising roughly 20–35% of total daily caloric intake for most adults. More important than the total, according to current nutritional guidance, is optimising the *type* of fat.

Practical guidelines worth following:

- Replace saturated fat sources with unsaturated alternatives where possible — using olive oil instead of butter, choosing nuts and avocado over high-fat processed snacks
- Aim to eat fatty fish two or more times per week to support omega-3 intake
- Check ingredient labels for "partially hydrogenated oil" and avoid products that contain it
- Whole food fat sources (nuts, seeds, fatty fish, avocado, olive oil) are generally preferable to processed fat sources, regardless of fat type, because they come alongside fibre, micronutrients, and other beneficial compounds

## The bottom line

Dietary fat is essential, nuanced, and worth understanding well beyond the simplified "fat is bad" messaging that shaped much of the late 20th century. Prioritise unsaturated fats — especially omega-3-rich sources — moderate saturated fat intake, and avoid industrial trans fats. These three principles, applied consistently over time, represent the evidence-based approach to dietary fat supported by organisations including the Mayo Clinic, the American Heart Association, and the World Health Organization.
