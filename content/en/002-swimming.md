# Why Swimming Might Be the Best Exercise Out There

Running, cycling, lifting weights — all are well-regarded exercises with genuine evidence behind them. But if one activity keeps surfacing in exercise science as offering an unusually complete package of physical and mental benefits, it's swimming. Whether you're a committed lap swimmer or someone who has never considered a pool seriously, the case for swimming as a near-ideal exercise is worth understanding.

## A full-body workout with minimal joint stress

Most forms of cardio load the body in one plane of movement. Running is highly effective but predominantly lower-body, and carries meaningful ground-reaction forces that accumulate over time — a significant contributor to overuse injuries in recreational runners.

Swimming engages virtually every major muscle group: the shoulders, back, core, hips, and legs all contribute to propulsion and stability in the water. Different strokes emphasise different muscles. Freestyle and backstroke challenge the lats and shoulders; breaststroke activates the chest, inner thigh, and glutes; butterfly, the most demanding stroke, recruits the entire posterior chain.

Critically, all of this happens in a buoyant, low-impact environment. Water reduces effective body weight by roughly 90%, dramatically lowering the mechanical stress on joints, bones, and connective tissue. This makes swimming particularly valuable for people managing joint conditions, recovering from injury, or looking for a high-intensity option that doesn't accumulate the same structural wear as land-based exercise.

## Cardiovascular and metabolic benefits

Swimming is among the most demanding cardiovascular exercises available. Sustained lap swimming at a moderate pace burns roughly 400–700 calories per hour depending on body weight and stroke intensity — comparable to running, but with substantially less orthopedic toll.

Regular swimming has been associated with reduced resting heart rate, lower blood pressure, improved cholesterol profiles, and better blood sugar regulation. In large prospective studies, swimmers have been found to have a significantly lower risk of all-cause mortality compared to sedentary individuals, comparing favourably even against walkers and runners on some measures.

The cardiovascular challenge in swimming is partly explained by the horizontal body position, which alters circulation dynamics and increases cardiac workload relative to upright exercise, and partly by the thermal environment — water conducts heat away from the body far faster than air, requiring greater metabolic effort to maintain core temperature.

## Lung capacity and breathing efficiency

Swimming places unique demands on the respiratory system. Breathing must be timed precisely to stroke rhythm, which trains respiratory muscles differently than most other forms of exercise. Competitive swimmers consistently demonstrate above-average lung capacity and respiratory efficiency. Regular recreational swimming has also been shown to improve breathing function in people with asthma, making it one of the few intense aerobic activities often recommended for that population.

## Mental health and wellbeing

"Swimming provides an array of specific benefits for both physical and mental health and well-being," notes Mitch Lomax, an exercise physiologist whose research focuses on swimming and human performance.

The mental health dimension is increasingly well-supported in the literature. Swimming has been associated with reduced symptoms of anxiety and depression, improved mood, and measurable reductions in stress. This likely reflects a combination of the aerobic endorphin response common to vigorous exercise, the meditative quality of rhythmic breath-focused movement, and the sensory distinctiveness of the aquatic environment itself.

Cold or cool water swimming has attracted particular research interest for its apparent effects on mental resilience and mood. While the evidence base is still developing, repeated cold water immersion appears to engage stress-adaptation pathways that some researchers associate with reduced anxiety and improved emotional regulation over time.

## Who can benefit — including people who can't do other exercises

One of swimming's most significant practical advantages is its accessibility to populations who struggle with land-based exercise. Individuals with arthritis, chronic back pain, lower-limb injuries, obesity, or mobility limitations often find swimming to be one of the few forms of vigorous exercise they can perform comfortably and consistently.

For older adults, swimming offers the cardiovascular and muscular benefits of high-effort exercise while eliminating the fall risk and joint stress that make many other activities problematic. For people during pregnancy, the buoyancy and temperature-regulating properties of water make pool exercise particularly well-tolerated across all trimesters.

## Getting started

You don't need to swim competitively or cover large distances to gain meaningful benefit. Even 20–30 minutes of continuous swimming two to three times per week represents a significant aerobic training stimulus.

If you are new to lap swimming:

- Start with a stroke you're comfortable with, even if it isn't the most efficient
- Focus on continuous movement rather than pace — staying in motion is the initial goal
- Consider a single session with a coach or more experienced swimmer to refine your technique; poor technique in swimming is unusually energy-costly and can contribute to shoulder strain over time

Most public pools offer lane swimming at accessible times. Many also offer guided aquatic fitness classes, which are excellent options for people who find lap swimming tedious or technically daunting.

## The bottom line

Swimming offers an unusual combination: full-body muscular engagement, significant cardiovascular demand, very low joint stress, meaningful mental health benefits, and accessibility to people who cannot safely perform many other forms of vigorous exercise. It is, in many respects, a complete exercise — one of the few activities where the evidence for broad health benefits is consistently strong across multiple dimensions simultaneously.
