The first thing worth knowing about tracking calories and macros is that everyone is estimating — including the food labels you read. Under U.S. FDA rules, the calorie figure on a package can be off by up to 20% and still pass inspection, and restaurant menu estimates are typically wider still. Even laboratory bomb-calorimetry, the most precise measurement of food energy available, doesn't capture how much of that energy your specific body actually absorbs. The goal of tracking, then, isn't to log a perfect number. It's to log a *consistent* number, so that you can see real patterns in your eating over weeks rather than chasing precision that doesn't exist.

With that frame in place, the work becomes much simpler. You need two things: a reasonable estimate of how much of a food you ate, and a reasonable estimate of what's in that food. This article walks through both, and points to the lookup sources you should reach for when Roundful doesn't have something on hand.

## Estimating portion size

Portion size is the larger source of error for most people, by a wide margin. Studies in which participants are asked to estimate the volume or weight of foods on a plate routinely find errors of 20–50% in both directions, especially for energy-dense foods like oils, nuts, cheese, and pasta. Three methods, in roughly increasing order of accuracy:

**Hand portions.** Your hand is always with you, scales reasonably to your body size, and is good enough for most situations. A common system:

- **Palm** = one serving of protein (~85–115 g cooked)
- **Cupped hand** = one serving of carbohydrates (~25–35 g cooked grains, ~150 g cooked vegetables)
- **Fist** = one serving of vegetables (~150 g)
- **Thumb** = one serving of fats (~10–15 g of oils, nut butters, hard cheese)

This is rough on purpose — it won't separate 130 g of chicken from 150 g of chicken — but it's reliable enough to estimate a full meal in under a minute, which matters more than another 15 g of precision you'd lose by skipping the log entirely.

**Household-object analogs.** Useful when the food doesn't sit neatly in your hand. A medium apple is roughly a tennis ball; a serving of cooked rice is roughly a baseball; a tablespoon of peanut butter is roughly a poker chip; a 30 g portion of cheese is roughly two dice; a 100 g sweet potato is roughly a computer mouse.

**A food scale.** The single biggest accuracy upgrade you can make. A basic digital kitchen scale costs less than a meal out and removes guesswork for the foods where guessing is least forgiving — oils, nut butters, granola, cooked rice, pasta, cheese. You don't need to weigh everything; weighing one or two of your most-eaten high-density foods catches the bulk of the error.

A subtle point worth flagging: nutrition databases usually list foods *raw* or *cooked* — but not both — and weight changes meaningfully when food is cooked. 100 g of raw chicken breast becomes about 75 g cooked; 100 g of dry rice becomes about 300 g cooked. Check which form the database entry refers to and weigh accordingly. Getting this wrong is one of the most common silent errors in tracking.

## Estimating what's in the food

Once you have a portion estimate, you need a number for what's in it. In order of how much you should trust them:

1. **Government nutrition databases** (USDA, Health Canada). Lab-validated, free, and the source most other databases ultimately copy from.
2. **Food labels on the package.** Subject to the ±20% rule, but a manufacturer-attested figure for that specific product.
3. **Restaurant chain nutrition pages.** Required by law for many large chains in the U.S., Canada, and UK. Reasonable for chains; less reliable for independent restaurants.
4. **Reputable nutrition apps and databases** (Roundful, Cronometer, MyFitnessPal). Generally good, but quality varies — most include crowdsourced entries that haven't been verified. Prefer entries marked as official, label-derived, or from a known database.
5. **Estimates from blogs, recipe sites, AI chatbots, or social media.** Use only if nothing else is available, and don't anchor any decisions to them.

## Where to look things up, beyond Roundful

When the food isn't in your tracker — or the entry looks suspicious — these are the sources worth bookmarking:

**[USDA FoodData Central](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/)** — the gold standard for unbranded, raw, and minimally processed foods. Free, searchable, and includes the SR Legacy database that most consumer apps source from. The "Foundation Foods" subset is the most rigorously verified.

**[Health Canada — Canadian Nutrient File (CNF)](https://food-nutrition.canada.ca/cnf-fce/index-eng.jsp)** — the Canadian equivalent. Useful for foods sold under Canadian labelling, and for figures that reflect Canadian agricultural and processing norms (which can differ subtly from U.S. ones, particularly for dairy and beef).

**Restaurant chain nutrition pages.** Most large chains in Canada, the U.S., the UK, and Australia publish a downloadable PDF or interactive table. Search "[chain name] nutrition information." For chains operating in B.C., the figures published on the parent corporate site are typically more complete than what you'll see posted in-store.

**[Open Food Facts](https://world.openfoodfacts.org/)** — a community-maintained database of packaged foods globally. Excellent coverage of imported and regional products that don't appear in U.S./Canadian databases. Treat entries with the same caution as any crowdsourced source: cross-check against the label when possible.

**[NHS Eatwell Guide](https://www.nhs.uk/live-well/eat-well/) and the [British Nutrition Foundation](https://www.nutrition.org.uk/)** — strong general-purpose UK references for portion guidance and dietary norms.

**The package label itself.** Trust the label over almost any database entry for branded products, since manufacturers are legally accountable for what they print. Note that serving sizes on labels are not always realistic — a "serving" of cereal is often half of what people actually pour.

A note on AI chatbots: they can be a reasonable starting point for generic foods ("how many calories in a medium banana"), but they should not be your primary source. Their answers come from training data of mixed quality, and they sometimes produce confident numbers for foods they don't actually have good data on. Always cross-check anything that surprises you.

## Common silent errors

A short checklist of the mistakes that quietly compound:

- **Cooking oil.** A tablespoon of olive oil is about 120 calories. If you cook with two tablespoons most days and don't log them, that's roughly 1,500 untracked calories a week.
- **Raw vs. cooked weight.** See above — easily a 25–50% error if you grab the wrong database entry.
- **Sauces, dressings, and condiments.** A heavy hand with peanut sauce, salad dressing, or mayonnaise can add 200–400 calories that don't feel like "food."
- **Drinks.** Juice, sweetened coffee, alcohol, and smoothies are easy to underestimate or skip. Alcohol in particular is often missed entirely.
- **"Bites and tastes."** A handful of nuts while cooking, a sip of someone else's drink, a piece of cheese while chopping — these can add up to several hundred calories on a busy day.
- **Restaurant servings.** Most restaurant entrees are 1.5 to 3 times the portion size you'd serve yourself at home. The published nutrition number is for the full plate, not "a reasonable amount."

## How to actually use all this

A practical approach for someone who wants reasonable tracking without it becoming a second job:

1. Use Roundful for most foods. Most days, the in-app numbers are accurate enough.
2. Reach for an external database when something is missing, looks wrong, or matters disproportionately (a food you eat daily, or a calorie-dense food).
3. Weigh the three or four highest-density foods you eat most often. Eyeball the rest.
4. Don't chase precision below 5–10%. The day-to-day variance in how your body actually absorbs and uses energy is wider than that.
5. Watch the weekly trend, not the daily number. One day of imperfect logging means nothing in isolation; seven days in a row of *consistent* logging tells you what's actually happening.

## The bottom line

Estimating calories and macros well is less about precision and more about avoiding systematic error: weighing the foods that matter, picking trustworthy lookup sources, and catching the things that get silently missed. Done consistently, even rough tracking surfaces patterns that no amount of intuition does — which is the entire point. Roundful is one tool in this; USDA FoodData Central, Health Canada's CNF, and the food label in your hand are the others worth keeping close.
