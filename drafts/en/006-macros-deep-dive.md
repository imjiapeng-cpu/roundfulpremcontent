The [Macros 101 article](005-macros-101.md) covered what the three macronutrients are and what each one does. This one goes a layer deeper: how much of each you actually need, how that shifts with your goals and activity level, how macros interact with calorie balance, and the myths worth retiring. By the end, you should have a working frame for setting your own macro targets — without having to copy somebody else's split off the internet.

## The starting point: AMDRs

The U.S. National Academies publish Acceptable Macronutrient Distribution Ranges (AMDRs) — the range of intake for each macronutrient that the evidence supports for general adult health:

- **Protein**: 10–35% of total daily calories
- **Carbohydrates**: 45–65% of total daily calories
- **Fat**: 20–35% of total daily calories

These ranges are intentionally wide. They reflect that healthy populations around the world thrive across very different macro splits — traditional Mediterranean diets sit high on fat (often 35%+), East Asian dietary patterns tend higher on carbs, athletic populations tend higher on protein. The AMDRs are floors and ceilings, not a prescription.

For most people, the most useful way to use them is in reverse: instead of starting from a calorie total and slicing it into percentages, start with a protein target in grams (covered next), then let carbs and fat fill in the remaining calories according to preference, satiety, and what you actually like eating.

## Protein in depth

Protein is the macronutrient where the gram-based target matters more than the percentage. This is because protein requirements scale with body size and activity level, not with calorie intake.

**General recommendations:**

- **Sedentary adults**: the Recommended Dietary Allowance (RDA) is 0.8 g of protein per kg of body weight per day. This is a *minimum to avoid deficiency*, not an optimum.
- **Active adults and recreational exercisers**: roughly 1.2–1.6 g/kg/day. This is well above the RDA but supported by the broader nutrition literature for people who train regularly.
- **Strength and endurance athletes**: the International Society of Sports Nutrition recommends 1.4–2.0 g/kg/day, with the higher end for those in a calorie deficit or pursuing meaningful muscle gain.
- **Older adults (65+)**: research increasingly supports 1.2–1.5 g/kg/day to offset age-related muscle loss (sarcopenia), even for those who aren't particularly active.

A worked example: a 70 kg (155 lb) recreational lifter, in maintenance calories, might target 70 × 1.6 = 112 g of protein per day. Spread across three or four meals, that's roughly 28–37 g per meal — about the protein in a palm-sized portion of chicken, fish, or tofu.

**Protein quality and amino acids.** Protein is built from 20 amino acids; nine are *essential* — your body can't make them and must get them from food. "Complete" proteins (most animal sources, soy, quinoa) contain all nine in usable proportions. Most other plant proteins are incomplete in isolation but become complete in combination. A varied plant-based diet that includes legumes, grains, soy, nuts, and seeds easily meets essential amino acid needs.

**Leucine and the MPS threshold.** Of the essential amino acids, leucine is the one most directly involved in triggering muscle protein synthesis (MPS) — the process by which your body builds muscle tissue. Most evidence suggests roughly 2.5–3 g of leucine per meal is enough to maximally stimulate MPS in healthy adults. In practical terms, this works out to about 25–30 g of high-quality protein per meal for a typical adult, more for older adults whose anabolic response is somewhat blunted.

**Distribution across the day.** Spreading protein across three to four meals appears to support muscle protein synthesis better than concentrating it in one or two large meals. The total over the day still matters most, but if you can hit your number across meals rather than one large dinner, the evidence slightly favours that approach.

**Is high protein bad for kidneys?** This is one of the most persistent myths. In healthy adults with normal kidney function, high-protein diets (within the ranges above) have not been shown to cause kidney damage. The concern is real for people with pre-existing kidney disease, who should work with a physician. For everyone else, this is a non-issue at the intakes discussed here.

## Carbohydrates in depth

Where protein is gram-based, carbohydrates are best thought of in terms of *quality* and *fit to your activity*.

**Quality first.** The single most useful split among carbohydrate sources is whole-food vs. heavily processed:

- **Whole-food carbs** (vegetables, fruit, beans, whole grains, oats, tubers) — high in fibre, digested more slowly, supportive of stable energy and gut health. Strong evidence base for long-term health benefits.
- **Refined and added-sugar carbs** (soft drinks, white bread, pastries, most breakfast cereals, candy) — digested rapidly, low fibre, low satiety per calorie. Reasonable in small amounts; problematic as a dominant share of carbohydrate intake.

The glycemic index (GI) and glycemic load (GL) measure how quickly a food raises blood sugar. They're useful as a rough orientation — lower-GL foods tend to support steadier energy — but they're an imperfect tool. The GL of an isolated food shifts dramatically when eaten with protein, fat, or fibre, which most foods are.

**Fibre.** Fibre is a carbohydrate the body can't digest, and it matters more than most people realise. Recommended intake is roughly 25 g/day for women and 38 g/day for men in the U.S.; Canadian guidelines are similar. Most adults eat less than half of this. Higher fibre intake is associated with lower cardiovascular disease, lower type 2 diabetes risk, better gut health, and improved appetite regulation.

**Activity-matched carbs.** Carbohydrate needs are highly dependent on what you're asking your body to do:

- **Sedentary or light activity**: AMDR range (45–65%) is wide enough that most patterns work
- **Moderate exercise (30–60 min/day)**: roughly 3–5 g/kg/day total carbs is a reasonable starting point
- **Endurance training (1–3 hr/day)**: 6–8 g/kg/day or higher; carbs become a meaningful performance variable
- **Strength training**: lower end of the active range is usually sufficient; carbs support training intensity and recovery but aren't as central as for endurance work

**Carbs at night.** Despite the persistent claim, eating carbs in the evening does not specifically cause fat gain. What matters is the total calorie balance over the day and week. There's actually modest evidence that consuming some carbohydrates in the evening can support sleep quality and recovery for trained individuals.

## Fat in depth

The [dietary fat article](003-dietary-fats.md) is the deeper treatment; here's the short version of what to know for macro planning:

- **AMDR**: 20–35% of calories. Below ~15% becomes a problem for hormone production and fat-soluble vitamin absorption; above 35% is fine if the sources are right.
- **Type matters more than total.** Prioritise unsaturated sources (olive oil, avocado, nuts, fatty fish). Moderate saturated fat. Avoid industrial trans fats.
- **Essential fats.** Omega-3 and omega-6 fatty acids must come from food. Aim for fatty fish two or more times per week, or plant sources of ALA (flax, chia, walnuts) with the understanding that conversion to EPA/DHA is limited.
- **Cooking matters.** Adding a tablespoon of oil to a dish adds 120 calories; cooking methods like deep frying can multiply this. Often the simplest macro improvement is just measuring cooking oil.

## Setting your own targets

A simple, defensible approach:

1. **Estimate your calorie maintenance.** Use a calculator (the Mifflin-St Jeor formula is the most widely validated) or work backwards from your current weight trend over a few weeks of consistent eating.
2. **Adjust based on your goal.**
   - **Fat loss**: 10–20% below maintenance, sustained
   - **Maintenance**: at maintenance
   - **Muscle gain**: 5–15% above maintenance, with consistent resistance training
3. **Set protein in grams.** 1.2–2.0 g/kg/day depending on activity and goal (higher when training hard and/or in a deficit).
4. **Set fat as a percentage.** 20–35% of total calories, leaning toward the upper end if you prefer fattier foods and lower end if you prefer more carbs.
5. **Let carbs absorb the remainder.** Whatever calories remain after protein and fat are accounted for, allocate to carbohydrates.

A worked example: a 70 kg adult in a moderate fat-loss phase eating 2,200 calories.

- Protein: 70 × 1.8 = 126 g → 504 calories (23%)
- Fat: 30% of 2,200 = 660 calories → 73 g
- Carbs: 2,200 − 504 − 660 = 1,036 calories → 259 g (47%)

This is a starting frame. Adjust based on satiety, energy, training performance, and the trend on the scale. Macros are an experiment you run on yourself over weeks.

## Myths worth retiring

- **"Carbs make you fat."** Calorie surplus makes you fat. Carbs are easy to overconsume in processed form, but the macro itself isn't the problem.
- **"Fat makes you fat."** Same answer. Fat is calorie-dense, which is *one* reason it's easy to overshoot — not because it has some special fat-storing property.
- **"You can only absorb 30 g of protein at a time."** The body can digest and use more than 30 g of protein in a meal; the MPS-saturation point is around 25–30 g but absorption is not capped.
- **"Late-night eating causes weight gain."** Total daily calorie balance is what matters, not when you eat.
- **"You have to track macros to be healthy."** Most healthy people through history never tracked anything. Tracking is a useful tool for some goals and some seasons of life, not a permanent requirement.

## The bottom line

Macro planning isn't complicated once you have a frame. Start with protein in grams, set fat as a percentage you can live with, let carbs fill the remainder, and adjust based on what your body tells you over weeks of consistent eating. The numbers are a guide, not a verdict — and the right split is the one that supports your goals while feeling sustainable enough to actually maintain.

> **Medical-advice disclaimer.** The recommendations in this article apply to most healthy adults. If you have kidney disease, diabetes, thyroid conditions, a history of eating disorders, are pregnant or breastfeeding, or are managing any chronic condition affecting metabolism, the right macro split for you may differ meaningfully. Please work with a registered dietitian or your physician for personalised advice rather than relying on general guidance like this.
