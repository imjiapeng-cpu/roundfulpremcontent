If you've spent any time in nutrition apps, fitness content, or grocery store labelling, you've encountered the word "macros." It gets thrown around as if everyone already knows what it means. This article is for the version of you who has been politely nodding along — and for the version that does know, but wants a refresher grounded in what the research actually says.

The short version: macronutrients are the three nutrients your body needs in large amounts to function — protein, carbohydrates, and fat. Everything else in food is either a micronutrient (vitamins and minerals), water, fibre, or something with no nutritional role at all. Macros are the foundation of how your body gets energy, builds tissue, and runs the daily biochemistry of being alive.

## What "macronutrient" actually means

The word splits into two halves. *Macro* — large. *Nutrient* — something the body needs from food. Macronutrients are the nutrients you need in gram-level quantities every day, as opposed to micronutrients (vitamins, minerals), which you need in milligram or microgram quantities.

Three macronutrients exist: protein, carbohydrates, and fat. A few sources also count alcohol as a fourth (it contains calories and is metabolised, but it serves no nutritional role and your body cannot store it usefully), and water is sometimes called a macronutrient under broader definitions. For practical tracking purposes, the relevant three are protein, carbs, and fat.

Each macronutrient provides energy, measured in calories (or kilojoules — 1 calorie ≈ 4.184 kJ):

- **Protein**: 4 calories per gram
- **Carbohydrates**: 4 calories per gram
- **Fat**: 9 calories per gram
- *(Alcohol, for reference: 7 calories per gram)*

This is why fat is so calorie-dense — it packs more than twice the energy per gram of the other two. It's also why "low-fat" diets often succeed at calorie reduction even when food volume stays the same.

## Protein: the body's building material

Protein is what your body uses to build, repair, and maintain itself. Skin, muscle, hair, nails, organs, enzymes, hormones, antibodies, neurotransmitters — all of these are built largely or entirely from protein. Unlike fat, the body has very limited capacity to store protein; what you don't use day to day gets broken down and either burned for fuel or excreted.

This is why daily protein intake matters more consistently than the other two macronutrients. Skip carbs for a meal — most people will be fine. Skip fat for a meal — also fine. Skip protein across many days in a row and the body starts cannibalising existing tissue (muscle, primarily) to meet ongoing maintenance demands.

Other roles worth knowing:

- **Satiety.** Of the three macronutrients, protein is the most filling per calorie, and the most likely to suppress subsequent appetite.
- **Thermic effect.** Roughly 20–30% of the calories in protein are burned just digesting it — the highest of the three macronutrients. Carbs sit around 5–10%; fat at 0–3%.
- **Immune and hormone function.** Antibodies, insulin, growth hormone, and many other regulatory molecules are proteins.

Sources include meat, fish, eggs, dairy, legumes, soy, tofu, tempeh, seitan, nuts, seeds, and some grains. Animal sources are generally "complete" — they contain all nine essential amino acids in usable proportions — while most plant sources are incomplete on their own but become complete in combination (rice and beans, hummus and pita).

## Carbohydrates: the body's primary fuel

Carbohydrates are the body's preferred energy source for most activities, particularly higher-intensity ones. When you eat carbs, your body breaks them down into glucose, which either circulates in the blood as fuel or gets stored as glycogen in the liver and muscles for later use.

A few specific things rely on carbohydrates more than the other two macros:

- **The brain.** Your brain uses roughly 120 g of glucose per day at rest, and it strongly prefers glucose to other fuel sources. It can switch to ketones (derived from fat) under prolonged carbohydrate restriction, but glucose is the default.
- **High-intensity exercise.** Anaerobic and high-intensity aerobic exercise rely heavily on muscle glycogen — the stored form of carbohydrate.
- **Recovery.** Replenishing glycogen after hard training is largely a carbohydrate-dependent process.

Carbs come in three broad forms: simple sugars (glucose, fructose, sucrose — fruit, table sugar, honey), complex carbohydrates (starches — grains, potatoes, legumes), and fibre (a non-digestible carbohydrate that supports gut health and digestion). The "carbs are bad" framing common in dieting culture is mostly about heavily processed simple-carb sources — soft drinks, refined sugar, white-flour pastries — not the category as a whole. Whole-food carbohydrate sources (vegetables, fruit, beans, whole grains) are associated with better health outcomes across nearly every dimension nutrition research has examined.

## Fat: structural and regulatory

Fat is the third macronutrient and the most calorie-dense. We covered this in depth in the [dietary fat article](003-dietary-fats.md); here's the short version of why your body needs it:

- **Cell membranes** — every cell is enclosed in a lipid bilayer
- **Hormone production** — sex hormones and steroid hormones are synthesised from cholesterol, a type of fat
- **Vitamin absorption** — vitamins A, D, E, and K are fat-soluble and require dietary fat to be absorbed
- **Brain function** — the brain is roughly 60% fat by dry weight
- **Energy storage** — body fat is the body's long-term energy reserve, and provides the primary fuel for sustained lower-intensity activity

The dietary-fat article covers the differences between saturated, unsaturated, and trans fats in detail. The short version: prioritise unsaturated sources (olive oil, avocado, nuts, fatty fish), moderate saturated fat, and avoid industrial trans fats.

## Why all three matter together

Diets that try to eliminate one macronutrient entirely tend to run into problems. Very-low-carb diets can impair high-intensity performance and, in some people, mood and sleep. Very-low-fat diets can disrupt hormone production and fat-soluble vitamin absorption. Very-low-protein diets compromise muscle, immune function, and recovery.

This isn't to say specific dietary approaches (ketogenic, vegan, Mediterranean, etc.) don't work — many do, for the right person and the right goal. It's to say that within any approach, you need enough of each of the three macronutrients to keep your body functioning well. The ratios are what shift between dietary patterns; the floor for each macronutrient stays in place.

## Calories vs. macros: which one matters?

Both, in different ways:

- **Calories** determine whether you gain, lose, or maintain weight. The total calorie balance over time is the single biggest driver of body weight change.
- **Macros** determine what *kind* of weight you gain or lose, how you feel day to day, and how well your body performs. Two people eating the same calorie total but very different macro splits will have meaningfully different body composition, energy, and performance outcomes.

A useful way to think about it: calories tell you the size of the budget. Macros tell you how you're spending it.

## The bottom line

Macros aren't a fitness-industry invention or a tracker-app gimmick. They're the three nutrient categories your body actually needs in large amounts to live, build tissue, and do hard things. Knowing roughly what each one does — and roughly what proportion of your diet each represents — turns calorie counting from a single noisy number into something you can actually act on.
